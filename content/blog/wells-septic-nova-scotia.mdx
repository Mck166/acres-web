A well and a septic system are not rustic charm. They are the water plant and the sewage plant for a [rural Nova Scotia](/blog/rural-property-nova-scotia) house. When they work, you stop thinking about them. When they fail, you think about them for a year and a pile of money. Municipal water and sewer hide this behind a bill. Off those services, you are the utility.

## Wells: dug, drilled, and the difference you can taste later

Nova Scotia has dug wells and drilled wells. A dug well is typically shallower and more influenced by surface conditions. A drilled well is typically deeper, with a casing, and a different set of mineral and yield questions. Listings that only say “well” are incomplete. Ask which kind, where it is, how old the pump is, and whether there is a pressure tank and any treatment (softener, UV, sediment, arsenic, manganese — treatment should match a lab result, not a neighbour’s rumour).

The province is clear that private well owners are responsible for their supply. Start with Nova Scotia’s [private wells](https://novascotia.ca/nse/water/privatewells.asp) pages and the current [well water testing](https://novascotia.ca/well-water-testing/) guidance. Those pages explain that you cannot tell safety by taste or smell, and they set out the province’s recommended testing rhythm for bacteria and for chemical contaminants. Use the government page for the current intervals and lab instructions. Do not treat a blog as the standard.

Under an offer, you want a current test done properly — sterile bottles, the lab’s instructions, a short trip to the drop-off — not a faded printout from 2017. If the seller has recent results, still consider testing yourself. You are the one who will drink it.

Yield matters as well as quality. A well that fills a kettle and fails when two people shower and the laundry runs is a lifestyle problem. Inspectors and well contractors can talk about recovery and pump performance. A dry August is a better teacher than a wet June viewing.

## What a water test is for (and what it is not)

A bacteria test looks for indicators such as total coliform and *E. coli*, following the lab’s method. A chemistry package looks at minerals, metals, and other measures that affect health or plumbing. Follow the province’s testing page for what to include rather than a shortcut panel someone invented.

A test is a snapshot. Flooding, a cracked cap, neglected treatment, or a long vacancy can change the next one. If you are buying a cottage that sat empty, budget for disinfection and a retest using provincial guidance, not folklore.

## Septic tanks, fields, and holding tanks

An on-site sewage disposal system — a septic tank plus a disposal field — is how many Nova Scotia homes treat wastewater where there is no central sewer. The province estimates that a large share of homes use these systems. Nova Scotia Environment and Climate Change regulates design, installation, and related trades. Start at the [on-site sewage disposal](https://novascotia.ca/nse/wastewater/on.site.sewage.disposal.asp) pages.

A typical system settles solids in a tank and sends liquid to a field where soil does the rest. If solids carry over because the tank was never pumped, the field can clog. The province recommends pumping on a schedule that depends on use; its sewage pages describe a common two-to-three-year pumping interval as a maintenance baseline. Confirm current guidance on the government site and match it to how many people will actually live in the house.

A holding tank is different. It stores sewage to be pumped and hauled. It is not a field. Operating cost and failure mode are different. If the listing says “septic” and the inspector finds a holding tank, that is not a synonym. It is a different property.

Malfunction looks like soggy ground over the field, sewage odours, backups in the house, or lush grass in a stripe you will learn to hate. It can also be quiet until the washing machine runs. A [home inspection](/blog/home-inspections-nova-scotia) should include someone who will locate the tank, look for records, and tell you when a specialist or a camera is worth it. Pump-out receipts are gold. “We never had a problem” is not a receipt.

Installing, replacing, or altering a system is not a DIY weekend. Nova Scotia requires certified people for this work, and new or replacement systems go through provincial notification or approval. If your offer assumes a new field after closing, get a qualified person involved before you treat the quote as a rounding error.

## Rural infrastructure around the house

Wells and septic sit in a thicker web:

- **Driveway and access** for pump-out trucks, oil trucks, and a well contractor
- **Power** for the well pump — no electricity, no water, unless you have a generator
- **Ditches and culverts** that keep the field from sitting in a swamp
- **Setbacks** from wells, watercourses, and the ocean — more sensitive on [waterfront](/blog/waterfront-property-nova-scotia) lots
- **Shared systems** on some older subdivisions or family lots

[Nova Scotia Power](https://www.nspower.ca/) keeps the pump running; outage planning is part of rural water. A generator inlet is a practical feature, not a luxury, if the well is your only supply.

Lot layout matters. A field between the house and the harbour, a well downhill from a barn, or a tank under the only parking pad will show up when something needs digging. Walk the property with the seller’s sketch, then verify. Parcel maps show lots, not buried tanks.

## How to fold this into an offer

Do not waive water and septic questions because the house is pretty. A clean sequence:

1. Read the listing for municipal vs well vs septic vs holding tank.
2. Ask for well records, water tests, pump-out invoices, and any permits for the field.
3. Write conditions that give you time to inspect, test, and walk if the system is failing.
4. Use labs and contractors who work in this province, and follow the sampling instructions on the well-testing pages above.
5. Price a worst-reasonable repair before you fall in love with the deck.
