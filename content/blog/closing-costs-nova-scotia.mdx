Closing costs in Nova Scotia are the extra cheques that show up after you agree on a price. Buyers who only stretch for the down payment get surprised by deed transfer tax, legal fees, and a property-tax adjustment in the same week. Budget them early, while you are still looking at [homes for sale](/properties), not the night before closing.

Figures change by municipality, property type, and the professionals you hire. This is a map of the categories, not a quote.

## The costs almost every buyer pays

### Deed transfer tax (land transfer)

Nova Scotia municipalities charge a deed transfer tax on the purchase. The rate is set locally. [Halifax Regional Municipality](https://www.halifax.ca/) publishes its rate and how the tax is calculated; other towns and rural municipalities set their own. Your lawyer will collect this on closing.

Because the tax is a percentage of the price, a higher offer is not only more mortgage — it is more tax the same day.

### Legal fees and disbursements

A Nova Scotia real estate lawyer searches title, prepares documents, deals with the lender, and records the deed. You pay a professional fee plus disbursements (searches, recording, courier, tax on fees). Rural files, condos, and anything with a messy title cost more time.

Do not shop lawyers on price alone. A cheap closing that misses an easement is not cheap.

### Home inspection (and specialists)

A general inspection is standard. In Nova Scotia you may also want:

- WETT inspection for a wood stove
- Septic inspection and pump-out records
- Water test for a well (bacteria, and sometimes minerals)
- Oil-tank inspection or documentation of age

These are optional in the legal sense and mandatory in the “I live here in January” sense.

### Mortgage-related fees

If you need [CMHC](https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/) default insurance (typical with less than 20 percent down), the premium is often added to the mortgage rather than paid in cash, but it still costs you. Lenders may also charge appraisal, application, or discharge fees. Ask for a full list at pre-approval.

## Adjustments: the proration pile

On closing, you and the seller settle the bills that overlap ownership:

- Municipal property tax, based on [PVSC](https://www.pvsc.ca/) assessment and the municipality’s rate
- Condo fees, if any
- Fuel oil left in a tank
- Prepaid utilities or rent, for investment properties

If the seller has paid tax for the year and you close in October, you will reimburse a share. If they have not paid, you may see a debit. Ask the lawyer for an estimate when the closing date is set.

## Deposits are not extra spending — but they are timing

The deposit with your offer is part of the purchase price, held in trust. It is not a closing cost, but it is cash you must produce *before* closing, often within 24 hours of acceptance. Know where that money sits (savings versus RRSP versus a gift) before you write the number.

## Sample mental math (not a quote)

For a modest resale house, many buyers plan a few percent of the purchase price for cash-to-close *beyond* the down payment, with deed transfer tax as the largest municipal line. A Halifax purchase at a published municipal rate will not match a purchase in a town with a different rate. Condos add the estoppel / status certificate process. New construction can add HST complexity.

If that range would empty your emergency fund, the price is too high even if the bank says yes.

## Condos, rural houses, and new builds are not the same cheque

A Halifax condo closing may add status-certificate or estoppel costs and a sharper look at condo fees. A rural closing may add well tests, septic inspection, and more legal time if the title or access is messy. A new-construction closing can involve holdbacks, incomplete work, and HST treatment that a resale lawyer letter will not copy-paste.

When you compare two favorites on [Acres](/properties), compare the closing-cost *shape*, not only the price. Municipal water and a paved street are part of what you are buying, even if they never appear as a line on the offer.

## How this fits the rest of the purchase

Closing costs sit in the middle of [how to buy a house in Nova Scotia](/blog/how-to-buy-a-house-in-nova-scotia): after the offer is accepted, before keys. They are also why a “cheap” rural house can cost more on day one than a pricier city house with municipal water — wells, septic, and oil do not care about your purchase price.

While you browse, keep a running note on each favorite: heat type, water, sewer, and the tax bill. That note is more useful than another saved photo. [Browse listings](/properties) and [save favorites](/blog/how-to-browse-homes-with-acres) so you are comparing real addresses, not hypotheticals.

## Ask for a written estimate

Before you waive conditions, ask your lawyer for a closing-cost estimate using the actual price, municipality, and closing date. Ask your lender for the full list of fees. Then add a buffer for inspection findings you might not renegotiate.

*This article is general information, not legal, tax, or mortgage advice. Confirm current deed transfer tax rates with the municipality and your Nova Scotia lawyer.*
