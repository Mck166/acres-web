A home inspection in Nova Scotia is how you find out whether the listing’s photos survived contact with the climate. Freeze-thaw, wind, oil heat, wells, septic fields, and basements that sit below a wet yard produce a different report than a city condo in a milder province. The inspection is also a legal tool: if your offer is conditional on a satisfactory inspection, you can renegotiate or walk. If you waived that condition to “win,” the report is only a shopping list you already own.

This checklist is general information, not a substitute for a licensed or qualified inspector, a WETT technician, a septic installer, or a lawyer. Use it to ask better questions. Tie it to [how to buy a house in Nova Scotia](/blog/how-to-buy-a-house-in-nova-scotia), where conditions sit in the offer.

## Put the inspection in the offer

A typical Nova Scotia offer includes an inspection condition with a deadline. Your REALTOR, licensed through the [Nova Scotia Real Estate Commission](https://nsrec.ns.ca/), should draft language your lawyer is comfortable with. Points to nail down:

- How many days you have, including time for specialists (septic, well, oil, WETT)
- Whether “satisfactory to the buyer” is the standard (common) versus a narrower defect list
- Access: attic, crawlspace, outbuildings, oil tank, septic access lids
- What happens if the inspector cannot complete because of snow, belongings, or no power

Do not book the inspector for the last afternoon of the condition. Rural specialists book up. Weather cancels roofs.

If the seller provides an old report, read it as history, not as your inspection. Houses change. Tanks age.

## Hire someone who works this province

Ask whether the inspector regularly does:

- Rural wells and septic (not only HRM municipal services)
- Oil tanks, including where they are sitting and what they can actually see
- Wood-burning appliances (they may defer to WETT)
- Older and [heritage homes](/blog/heritage-homes-nova-scotia) — plaster, slate, stone foundations, knob-and-tube
- Coastal moisture, ice dams, and crawlspaces

A bargain inspection that skips the roof because it is raining is not a bargain. Attend if you can. The walk-through is when you learn which problems are “budget this year” versus “this well is not potable.”

## Oil tanks

Oil heat is still common. Tanks fail. Spills are expensive. Your checklist:

- Age of the tank (look for a date stamp; ask for invoices)
- Location: basement, outside, or underground (underground is a specialist and environmental problem)
- Visible rust, leaks, drip stains, abandoned fill pipes
- Whether the insurer or lender has age limits — many do
- Oil lines, filters, and the furnace or boiler the tank feeds

An oil-tank inspection or a tank replacement quote during conditions is normal. Do not accept “it has always been fine.” Lenders funding the deal may require a newer tank. Confirm current environmental and tank rules with provincial sources on [novascotia.ca](https://novascotia.ca/) and with your insurer; do not rely on a neighbour’s memory of a bylaw.

## Wells and water

If the house is not on municipal water, the well is the plumbing. During conditions:

- Bacteria test (and follow the lab’s sampling rules)
- Flow and recovery if the inspector or a well person can assess them
- Minerals, sulphur, iron, and treatment equipment already in place
- Pump, pressure tank, and whether there is water when the power is out
- Distance from septic, fuel, and livestock if relevant

A seller who drinks the water has not completed your test. Municipal wells and private wells are different files. HRM servicing maps live with the municipality ([halifax.ca](https://www.halifax.ca/) for Halifax-area properties). Outside towns, assume nothing.

No power, no well pump, no water. Storm outages are ordinary in rural Nova Scotia; ask about a generator if you are on a well.

## Septic systems

A septic tank and field sized for the original cottage may not match a three-bedroom listing. Ask for:

- Age, last pump-out, and any permits or repair records
- Location of tank and field (do not park or pave over it)
- A septic inspection and, when appropriate, a pump-out during the condition
- Bedroom count versus system design
- Surface breakout, wet spots, and odour in the yard

Replacement is a major cost. It is also a permit and setback problem on small lots. This is one of the main reasons “cheap rural” is not cheap.

## Roofs, ice, and water management

Nova Scotia roofs take wind, ice dams, and freeze-thaw. Look at:

- Age and remaining life; number of layers
- Flashing at chimneys, skylights, and valleys
- Attic ventilation, insulation, and staining
- Gutters, downspouts, and whether water is thrown away from the foundation
- Flat roofs and coastal metal roofs as their own products

The inspector may not walk a steep or icy roof. Budget for a roofer if the covering is old or the camera cannot see.

Inside, follow water: basement corners, around the oil tank, under toilets, around tubs. A finished basement can hide the evidence. Ask what the inspector could not see.

## Heat, wood stoves, and WETT

Heat source drives [heating costs in Nova Scotia](/blog/heating-costs-nova-scotia) and insurability.

- **Oil:** tank plus appliance condition
- **Electric baseboard:** panel capacity and the bill (ask for actual bills)
- **Heat pumps:** install quality, backup heat, and whether they are the only heat
- **Wood or pellet:** a **WETT** inspection is what many insurers want. A general home inspector may not certify the appliance. Book WETT inside the condition window if there is a wood stove or fireplace insert.

Chimneys, clearances, and a stove that was a previous owner’s weekend project fail insurance. Fail insurance, and the lender has a problem.

## Structure, electrical, and the rest of the list

Still walk:

- Foundation cracks, settlement, and crawlspace moisture
- Electrical panel, amperage, aluminum branch wiring, extension-cord life in the basement
- Windows, doors, and the envelope (especially on [heritage](/blog/heritage-homes-nova-scotia) buildings)
- Outbuildings, driveways, and shared wells or rights-of-way (title is the lawyer; the inspector notes what they see)
- Coastal flood and erosion clues if you are near water

Assessment and tax history from [PVSC](https://www.pvsc.ca/) do not replace an inspection. A PID lookup does not tell you the roof age. Use both, plus the inspector.

## After the report: negotiate or leave

The report is for you. Typical paths:

- Ask for repairs, a price reduction, or a credit
- Walk under the condition if the well, septic, tank, or structure is a deal-breaker
- Proceed if the issues are priced in and you still want the house

Do not nickel-and-dime a seller over a $40 tap while ignoring a tank. Do not ignore a failed water test because you like the kitchen. Your lawyer should see any amendment.

## Book the inspection around a real shortlist

Browse [listings](/properties) or the [map](/map), [save a few addresses](/blog/how-to-browse-homes-with-acres), and only then spend money on inspectors. A clean process is: offer with conditions, inspect, then decide — not inspect a house you have not been able to buy.
