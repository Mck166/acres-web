Home heating costs in Nova Scotia are not a footnote to the mortgage. They are a second bill that runs for months, through wind and freeze-thaw, in houses that were often built for a different fuel. Oil, ductless heat pumps, electric baseboard, and wood all show up in the same week of listings. If you compare kitchens and ignore heat, you will misread the price.

This article is about what to ask on a listing and at a viewing. It does not quote dollar amounts, because tanks, envelopes, and rates change, and because your neighbour’s house is not your house. Confirm programs and electricity details with [Nova Scotia Power](https://www.nspower.ca/) and with the professionals who will inspect the system.

Heating sits inside the broader [purchase checklist](/blog/how-to-buy-a-house-in-nova-scotia): it is one of the reasons a “cheap” house is not cheap.

## Read the heat source before you fall for the photo

Listings usually name the primary heat. Treat that line as the start of a conversation:

- **Oil** — furnace or boiler, plus a tank that has an age, a location, and an insurance story.
- **Ductless heat pump** — one or more wall heads, sometimes covering the main floor and leaving the basement or far bedrooms on another source.
- **Electric baseboard or furnace** — simple equipment, bills that follow the envelope and the rate.
- **Wood or pellet** — stove or furnace that insurers and WETT inspectors care about.
- **Mixed** — the most honest description in a lot of Nova Scotia houses.

“Forced air” and “hot water” tell you how heat moves. They do not tell you what you will pay. A tight bungalow on a heat pump and a leaky two-storey on baseboard can share a street and not share a winter.

If the listing is silent, ask. Silence is not a good sign. Save the property only if you are willing to inspect the system. [Favorites](/blog/how-to-browse-homes-with-acres) should include a note on heat the same way they include a note on price.

## Oil tanks are a condition item, not a background prop

Oil heat is still common, especially in older stock. The tank can be in the basement, outside, or, in older files, underground. Age, construction (single wall versus double wall), and location change insurance and replacement timing. A tank that is out of warranty, undocumented, or buried is a reason to slow down, not a reason to negotiate only on paint.

Ask:

- Where is the tank, and can you see it?
- Is there documentation of age, installation, or inspection?
- Has the insurer already flagged it?
- Is there staining or a smell that the inspector should treat as a contamination question?

Underground tanks are a specialist topic. Do not accept a shrug. Your lawyer and inspector should know when to bring in someone who deals with tanks for a living.

Oil left in the tank is often an adjustment on closing. That is a small line. A tank that has to be replaced before an insurer will bind is a large one. Budget a buffer in the same family of extras as [closing costs](/blog/closing-costs-nova-scotia), not as a maybe.

## Ductless heat pumps: ask what they actually heat

Mini-splits are widespread because they move heat efficiently in this climate when they are sized and installed for the house. They are also easy to market. A single head in the living room does not heat a 1960s split-entry the way a listing photo implies.

At the viewing, count the heads and the rooms. Ask whether the basement, the far bedrooms, and the upstairs still rely on baseboard or oil. Ask the age of the outdoor unit and whether the seller has service records. Ask how the house behaved in the last cold snap — not as a number, as a description. If the seller “doesn’t use the oil anymore,” find out whether the oil system still works and whether the tank is still a liability.

Heat pumps need electricity. Outages, which happen in coastal and rural Nova Scotia, mean you need a backup plan: a working secondary system, a generator strategy, or a house that will not freeze in twelve hours. [Waterfront and rural properties](/blog/waterfront-property-nova-scotia) make this more acute.

NS Power’s site is the right place for current efficiency programs, time-varying rates, and customer rules. Programs change. The listing will not keep up.

## Electric baseboard and the leaky envelope

Baseboard is simple and it is honest: you will feel the cost of poor insulation, single-pane windows, and air leaks. Many mid-century Dartmouth and Cape Breton houses still use it, sometimes with a heat pump added later. The combination can work. The combination can also mean you are paying for two systems and still wearing a sweater in the hall.

You cannot price this from the listing. You can:

- Look at window age and type
- Ask about insulation in the attic and basement
- Feel for drafts at outlets and sills
- Ask for a winter’s worth of electric bills if the seller will share them

Bills are not a guarantee of your future cost. They are better than a guess. If the seller will not share them, your inspector’s notes on insulation and air sealing matter more.

## Wood heat: useful, and not casual

A wood stove can take the edge off a power outage and reduce other fuel use. It also needs a WETT inspection for many insurers, clearances that match how the room is actually used, and a chimney that is not a fantasy. If wood is part of why the house “heats cheap,” put WETT and insurance on the condition list.

## What to write down for every listing

Keep a short field note:

1. Primary heat and any backup
2. Tank location and any documents
3. Number of heat-pump heads versus number of rooms
4. Wood appliance and whether WETT exists
5. Whether bills were provided

Then compare two favorites on the [map](/map) only after those notes exist. A cheaper house with electric baseboard and original windows may cost more to live in than a pricier house with a recent heat pump and a documented tank. You will not see that in the asking price.

Municipal property tax is a separate winter-adjacent bill; heating is the one that spikes when the wind comes off the water. Look up [NS Power](https://www.nspower.ca/) for the electricity side, and ask the oil company or the seller for delivery history if oil is in play. [Canada.ca](https://www.canada.ca/) is the place for federal energy-efficiency program overviews when those are part of your renovation plan — after you own the house, not as a reason to skip inspection.

When you [browse Nova Scotia listings](/properties), treat heat as a filter you apply with your eyes. Open the listing, find the fuel, and decide whether you understand it well enough to write an offer. If you do not, book a viewing with an inspector in mind, not a closing date.
