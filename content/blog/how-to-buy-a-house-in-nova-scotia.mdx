Buying a house in Nova Scotia is a sequence of clear steps: get your numbers in order, look at real listings, write an offer with conditions, and close with a lawyer. The legal system, taxes, and even the way properties are identified (by PID) are specific to this province, so it helps to know the local path before you fall in love with a photo.

This is a practical overview, not legal or mortgage advice. Confirm figures with a Nova Scotia lawyer, a lender, and the agencies linked below.

## 1. Know what you can actually spend

Start with a mortgage pre-approval, not a listing. A lender or mortgage broker will look at income, debts, down payment, and the federal stress test. The [Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation](https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/) explains when mortgage default insurance applies if your down payment is under 20 percent.

Your budget is not only the purchase price. In Nova Scotia you should also plan for:

- A deposit with the offer
- Deed transfer tax (municipal; Halifax publishes its own rate)
- Legal fees and disbursements
- Home inspection
- Adjustments on closing (property tax, oil in the tank, condo fees)
- Moving, immediate repairs, and the first winter’s heat

A useful rule: if the mortgage payment only works when you ignore heat, taxes, and a well pump, it does not work.

## 2. Look at live listings, not just sold data

Nova Scotia is not one market. A Dartmouth split-entry, a Halifax peninsula condo, a Lunenburg waterfront house, and a Cape Breton farmhouse close on different timelines and with different inspection issues.

Browse [current homes for sale](/properties) on Acres, or open the [map](/map) if you already care about a street or a commute. You can [browse listings and save favorites](/blog/how-to-browse-homes-with-acres) without an account; sign in when you want that shortlist on your phone and your laptop.

Pay attention to:

- **PID and lot size** — rural “acreage” is not always what the photos imply
- **Heat source** — oil, heat pump, electric baseboard, or mixed
- **Water and sewer** — municipal, well, septic
- **Age and updates** — wiring, roof, windows, and insulation matter in this climate
- **Drive time in winter**, not only in July

## 3. Work with licensed people

Most buyers in Nova Scotia work with a REALTOR licensed through the [Nova Scotia Real Estate Commission](https://nsrec.ns.ca/). You will also need a real estate lawyer to search title, handle the trust deposit, and close. If the property is rural, your inspector should be comfortable with wells, septic systems, and oil tanks — not only city condos.

You can tour homes before you hire anyone, but the offer, conditions, and closing should not be improvised.

## 4. View with a checklist, then write an offer

When you book a viewing, look past staging. Open the electrical panel, ask about the oil tank’s age, run water, and walk the grade around the foundation. Coastal and lakeside homes need extra attention for flood, erosion, and insurance.

An offer in Nova Scotia typically includes:

- Price and deposit
- Closing date
- Conditions — financing, inspection, and sometimes sale of the buyer’s current home
- Fixtures and chattels (what stays)
- A deadline for the seller’s response

Conditions exist so you can walk away if the inspection or the lender says no. Waiving them to “win” a bidding war is a financial decision, not a vibe.

## 5. Inspection, financing, and the lawyer’s search

If the inspection finds a failed septic field, an underground oil tank, or a roof at the end of its life, you can renegotiate, ask for repairs, or end the deal under the condition. Your lawyer will search title, check for easements and judgments, and confirm you are buying the lot you think you are buying.

Property tax in Nova Scotia is billed by the municipality using assessments from [Property Valuation Services Corporation (PVSC)](https://www.pvsc.ca/). Ask for the latest tax bill, not only the listing’s round number.

## 6. Close, then take the keys

On closing day, funds move through the lawyer’s trust account. You get keys when the deed is recorded and the lender is satisfied. Budget a buffer: oil tanks, well pumps, and first-year insurance surprises are more common here than a missing fridge.

After you own the place, keep the survey, the septic records, and the oil-tank invoice. The next buyer will ask.
