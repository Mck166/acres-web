The cost of owning a home in Nova Scotia is the mortgage plus everything that keeps the house legal, warm, dry, and insured after the realtor photos come down. Buyers who only stretch for the payment discover property tax, oil, power, and insurance in the same quarter, then call the house “more expensive than it looked.” It was always that expensive. The listing just did not add the lines.

This is a roundup, not a quote. Rates and premiums change. Use it as a checklist while you compare [live listings](/properties) on the [map](/map). [Save a short list](/blog/how-to-browse-homes-with-acres) and [sign up](/signup) so you are costing real addresses, not a hypothetical average house that does not exist.

## Start with the payment, then refuse to stop there

Your lender will talk about principal, interest, and the federal stress test. [CMHC](https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/) explains default insurance when the down payment is under 20 percent. That math is necessary. It is not sufficient.

On top of the mortgage, most owners should budget for:

- Municipal property tax
- Heat (oil, heat pump, electric, wood, or a mix)
- Electricity for everything that is not the furnace
- Home insurance
- Water and sewer, or well and septic maintenance
- Condo fees if you buy a unit
- Internet, and often a second vehicle outside the core
- Maintenance: roof, windows, paint, appliances, the unexpected January failure

If the payment only works when those lines are zero, the house does not work. That is true in Halifax and it is true on a dirt road.

## Property tax is local, assessment is provincial

[Property tax in Nova Scotia](/blog/nova-scotia-property-tax) is billed by the municipality using assessed values from [Property Valuation Services Corporation (PVSC)](https://www.pvsc.ca/). HRM is not the same mill rate as a small town. A waterfront lot is not the same assessment as a similar house inland. Ask for the latest tax bill, not the round number in the remarks.

Assessment is not market value. A low assessment can mean a bargain or a coming supplementary bill after renovations. A high assessment in Halifax can simply mean you bought in a high-value neighbourhood. When you compare two favorites, put last year’s tax next to last year’s heat. Those two columns explain more than granite.

Deed transfer tax is a purchase cost, not a year-round cost, but it hits the same bank account. Do not mix it into the monthly model twice.

## Heat is the bill that teaches newcomers

[Heating costs](/blog/heating-costs-nova-scotia) dominate winter conversations in this province for a reason. Oil, electric baseboard, ducted heat pumps, mini-splits, and wood all show up in listings. The cheap house with electric baseboard and original windows can cost more to occupy than a tighter house with a higher price.

Ask for fuel or power history. Visit in cold weather if you can. Note whether the listing’s “heat pump” is the whole house or one wall in the living room. Note the oil tank’s age because insurance and replacement sit on that timeline.

Wood is not free. It is labour, a dry supply, a WETT-inspected appliance, and a chimney. It can still be a rational rural system. It is not a rounding error of zero.

## Electricity is not only the lights

Even with oil or a heat pump, [Nova Scotia Power](https://www.nspower.ca/) still bills you. Water heaters, dryers, well pumps, baseboard backup, and winter lighting add up. [Electricity costs](/blog/nova-scotia-power-home-electricity-costs) deserve their own look because a rural well house with electric heat is a different account from a peninsula condo with a gas or oil plant you never see.

Outages are part of the operating story outside the densest grid. A generator is an ownership cost if the well cannot run without power. Time-of-day habits help; they do not replace insulation.

Use NS Power’s own tools and your own bills. Do not use a national average from a different climate.

## Insurance is annual, and sometimes a veto

[Home insurance](/blog/home-insurance-nova-scotia) is a yearly (or monthly) invoice and a condition of most mortgages. Older homes, coastal lots, oil tanks, and wood stoves change what companies will write. Premiums are quote-specific; this article will not invent them.

Get a broker to price the actual civic address while you still have conditions. A bindable policy is part of the cost of owning, not a closing-day surprise. Flood and water endorsements, if available, are extra questions on coastal and low lots — see also flood and coastal risk if that is your search.

## Water, sewer, and the systems behind the wall

Municipal water and wastewater are a bill. A well is tests, a pump, and treatment. A septic system is pumping and the possibility of a field replacement. Those are ownership costs even in years when nothing “breaks.” Skipping a pump-out to save money is how fields fail.

Condo fees bundle some of this and add the rest: reserve funds, insurance deductibles at the corporation level, special assessments. Read the documents. A low condo fee with a thin reserve is not a bargain; it is a future levy.

## Maintenance is a climate tax

Nova Scotia houses live in salt air, freeze-thaw, and wind. Roofs, decks, windows, and exterior paint do not last like they do in a milder cartoon of Canada. Older stock needs electrical and plumbing attention. Coastal hardware rusts. Driveways need gravel. Eavestroughs that fail will put water in the basement you just finished.

A useful habit is to keep a yearly envelope for “the house,” separate from groceries. The amount depends on age and condition. The envelope should not be zero.

## How to compare two houses on total cost

For each favorite:

1. Mortgage payment at your actual down payment and rate environment ([Bank of Canada](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/) for context, your lender for the number)
2. Property tax from the bill, checked against assessment records
3. Heat, using the listing type plus any history
4. Electricity, including well pump if rural
5. Insurance quote
6. Water/sewer or well/septic allowance
7. Condo fee or a maintenance allowance for a freehold

Then look at commute. A cheaper house that needs two cars and an hour on Highway 102 is not cheaper.
