Working with a REALTOR in Nova Scotia means hiring a licensed brokerage, not collecting informal texts from someone’s cousin who “knows the market.” The [Nova Scotia Real Estate Commission](https://nsrec.ns.ca/) regulates people who trade in real estate in this province. If you want the consumer protections that come with that system, you work with someone who is actually licensed.

You can look at homes before you sign anything. You should not improvise the offer. [How to buy a house in Nova Scotia](/blog/how-to-buy-a-house-in-nova-scotia) is a sequence; the licensee is the person who drafts the paper in the middle of it. Start your search on [listings](/properties) and the [map](/map), [save a short list](/blog/how-to-browse-homes-with-acres), and [sign up](/signup) so you walk into a first meeting with addresses, not vibes.

## The Commission is the regulator, not your agent

NSREC licenses brokerages and individuals, investigates complaints, and publishes consumer material. It is not a firm you hire to buy a house. Before you commit, use the Commission’s [licensee search](https://nsrec.ns.ca/) and its guidance on [choosing a licensee](https://www.nsrec.ns.ca/consumers/getting-started/choosing-a-licensee). A person who is not licensed does not bring the same legal framework with them. The Commission is blunt about that.

A licensee trades on behalf of a brokerage. The broker is responsible for supervision. Your contract is with the brokerage, even if you only ever talk to one salesperson. That sounds fussy until something goes wrong and you need to know who actually owed you a duty.

Ask about experience in the kind of property you want: peninsula condos, rural wells, new construction, or a specific town. A strong urban agent is not automatically a strong Cape Breton land agent. You are allowed to interview more than one person.

## Client, customer, and the paperwork you will be handed

Early on, you should see disclosure about how the industry works — in Nova Scotia this is commonly framed around whether you are a client of the brokerage or an unrepresented party. Those statuses are not the same. A client relationship comes with defined obligations. An unrepresented buyer being shown a seller’s listing is in a different position.

NSREC explains [buyer brokerage agreements](https://www.nsrec.ns.ca/consumers/getting-started/about-buyer-brokerage-agreements). In this province, buyer clients work under written brokerage agreements. Read the term, the services, and how remuneration is handled before you sign. Duration is negotiable in the sense that you agree to a period; it is not “until I feel like leaving” if the contract says otherwise. The Commission warns that walking away mid-agreement is not always simple. Choose someone you can work with for months, not someone who is merely available this afternoon.

If a promise is important — evening viewings, a certain search area, help with rural due diligence — get it in the agreement. Verbal extras evaporate.

You do not have to become a client to look at a house once. You do have to understand which hat the person beside you is wearing when they start talking about price.

## What a good buyer’s agent actually does here

A useful REALTOR in Nova Scotia does more than unlock doors:

- Helps you interpret listings: heat, PID, water, sewer, and “as is”
- Books viewings and keeps you from touring fifty maybes
- Drafts offers with conditions that match this province’s practice
- Negotiates deadlines, inclusions, and repairs without theatre
- Coordinates with your lawyer and, when needed, the listing brokerage
- Knows when a specialist (septic, WETT, oil tank) should be in the file

They do not replace a lawyer, an inspector, or a lender. They also do not replace your own homework on [offers, conditions, and closing timelines](/blog/offers-conditions-closing-timeline-nova-scotia). If someone tells you to waive the inspection “because that’s how you win in Halifax,” that is a strategy pitch, not a law of physics. Sometimes inventories are tight. Tight is not the same as “conditions are for cowards.”

Bring your own shortlist from [Acres](/properties). A licensee who is worth the relationship will work with the homes you already care about, and will tell you when a pin is a bad idea, not only when it is a good one.

## Viewings, dual agency, and talking in the driveway

Do not critique the roof in the living room. Do not announce your maximum price in front of the listing agent. Do not treat an open house as a negotiation seminar.

If the same brokerage represents both sides, or if designated agency is in play, you should get a clear explanation of who owes what to whom. Ask until you understand. “We’ll be fair” is not a structure. NSREC’s consumer pages exist because this confusion is common.

On the viewing itself, look at the house, not the staging. A licensee who knows Nova Scotia will point you at the panel, the tank, the grade, and the crawlspace, and will not rush you because the next showing is in twelve minutes. If they rush you anyway, that is data about how they work.

## Offers are where the licence matters

Anyone can have an opinion about price. A licensee’s job is to put your terms on a form that the other side can accept, reject, or counter, with deposits, dates, and conditions that actually function. Financing, inspection, and sometimes the sale of your current home are the usual levers. The deposit is real money on a real timeline.

Your REALTOR should not pressure you to skip a lawyer. Title, trust funds, and closing are the lawyer’s lane. The two professionals should be able to share a file without drama.

If you are buying from another province, insist on video walkthroughs plus at least one trusted local set of eyes. A licence in Nova Scotia is local for a reason. Land, wells, and winter access do not translate from Ontario suburbia by Zoom alone.

## How to start without getting captured

A clean start:

1. Browse [properties](/properties) and the [map](/map) until you have a handful of serious addresses.
2. [Create an account](/signup) and keep [favorites](/blog/how-to-browse-homes-with-acres) tight.
3. Verify any name on the Commission site.
4. Interview, then sign a buyer brokerage agreement you have actually read.
5. View, inspect, and offer with a lawyer already chosen.

You can be a thorough shopper and still hire help. Acres is for looking at live Nova Scotia inventory. A licensed brokerage is for trading. Use both. Do not confuse a saved heart with representation, and do not confuse a friendly showing with a fiduciary relationship you never agreed to.
