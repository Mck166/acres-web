Home insurance in Nova Scotia is not a form you fill after you get the keys. It is one of the conditions that can quietly kill a deal, especially on older houses, coastal lots, oil heat, and anything a broker has seen flood before. Photos do not underwrite a policy. Insurers ask about the roof, the wiring, the oil tank, the wood stove, the distance to a fire hydrant, and whether the house sits where water has a habit of arriving.

Premiums vary by insurer, deductible, claims history, construction, and location. This article does not quote prices, because a number invented on a blog is worse than no number. ## Bind insurance before you are emotionally committed

Lenders require insurance. Even a cash buyer should not close uninsured. In practice, you (or your broker) should be able to bind a policy effective on closing day, with the lawyer and the lender named as needed.

Start the conversation as soon as an offer is accepted. Send the listing, the year built, the heat type, and any inspection notes. If the house is a [fixer-upper](/blog/fixer-uppers-nova-scotia), say so. If it is [waterfront](/blog/waterfront-property-nova-scotia), say so. Surprises at the underwriting desk are how people end up scrambling three days before closing.

A broker who works this province regularly will know which markets are fussy about knob-and-tube, galvanized plumbing, uncertified wood stoves, and aging oil tanks.

## Older homes: wiring, roofs, and “character”

Nova Scotia’s housing stock includes wartime houses, farmhouses, peninsula homes with additions, and rural places that were cottages first. Insurers care about what fails in a fire or a leak.

Expect questions about:

- Electrical service and panel (amp size, fuses vs breakers, aluminum or knob-and-tube)
- Roof age and covering
- Plumbing type and any known leaks
- Occupancy — owner-occupied vs vacant vs under renovation
- Claims on the property, if known

“Updates throughout” on a listing is not an electrical certificate. A [home inspection](/blog/home-inspections-nova-scotia) can flag panels and wiring; an electrician may still need to speak if the insurer wants confirmation. Vacant homes and long renovations are often a different product than a normal homeowner policy. If you plan to gut the place, tell the broker before you assume last year’s policy type still applies.

Heritage character is a tax and permit topic as much as an insurance topic. Decorative plaster does not make a house uninsurable. Open-flame wiring and a roof at the end of its life can.

## Oil tanks are an underwriting item, not a backyard detail

Oil heat is still common, especially outside the core of Halifax. Insurers typically want to know whether the tank is above ground or buried, indoors or outdoors, and how old it is. Buried tanks and tanks past an insurer’s age cutoff are frequent deal problems. Some companies will not write a risk with an underground tank. Others will, with conditions you should read.

Do not guess the tank’s age from a coat of paint. Ask for install records. Put tank inspection or replacement in the same mental bucket as [heating costs](/blog/heating-costs-nova-scotia): it is part of owning the system, not a surprise add-on.

A leak is both an environmental problem and an insurance problem. Nova Scotia Environment’s world and your insurer’s world both care. If the listing is silent on the tank and the house is clearly on oil, treat that silence as a to-do, not as comfort.

## Wood stoves, WETT, and the chimney

Wood heat is normal in rural Nova Scotia. It is also a common reason a policy is delayed. Many insurers want a WETT inspection (Wood Energy Technology Transfer) showing the appliance and chimney installation meet the standard they require. A pretty stove in the listing photos is not that document.

If you love the stove, budget time for WETT and possible work on clearances, lining, or the chimney. If you do not plan to use it, say so — and do not assume “we will just not light it” satisfies an underwriter if the appliance is still installed.

Pellet stoves and outdoor wood boilers have their own quirks. Name the appliance.

## Coastal, flood, and what a home policy may not cover

A standard homeowner policy is not a complete answer to storm surge, overland flood, or erosion. Coverage for sewer backup, overland water, and flood-related damage is highly product-specific in Canada. Some endorsements exist. Some risks are excluded or limited. Coastal erosion is often a land problem, not a rebuild-the-house problem.

If you are looking at [waterfront property](/blog/waterfront-property-nova-scotia) or a low lot near a harbour, a lake, or a river, ask the broker in plain language: what is covered if water comes in from the outside, from the sewer, or from the ground? Then read the answer twice.

The province’s [coastal climate pages](https://novascotia.ca/coastal-climate-change/) are a better first look at physical risk than a listing’s sunset photo. [Halifax](https://www.halifax.ca/) publishes municipal flood-mapping information for HRM. Neither website sells you a policy. Both tell you whether you should be asking harder insurance questions.

Claims history matters. Prior water claims may change the company, the deductible, or whether anyone will write the risk. You will not see that in the remarks. Your broker might, if they ask.

## Distance to fire service, liability, and the rural extras

Rural properties are sometimes rated for distance to a hydrant or a fire hall. That can change the premium or the company that will write the risk.

Other questions that come up in this province:

- Short-term rentals and home-based businesses
- Secondary suites and basement apartments
- Trampolines, certain dogs, and other liability hobbies
- Unoccupied seasonal cottages vs year-round homes

Be honest. An undisclosed short-term rental is a claims problem later. A legal secondary suite may be fine; an informal basement rental may not be.

## Build insurance into the offer timeline

When you write the offer, you already have financing and inspection conditions in mind. Add a practical insurance check to the same window. If the broker cannot place the risk without a new roof, a tank replacement, or a WETT certificate, you want that news before you are unconditional.

A sensible loop:

1. Shortlist on [Acres](/properties) and confirm the lot on the [map](/map).
2. Ask heat, tank, stove, and coastal questions from the listing.
3. Get a broker looking at the address as soon as the offer is accepted.
4. Let inspection findings feed the insurance conversation.
5. Only then waive conditions.

Insurance is how you live with a Nova Scotia house after a bad night of wind. It is not a closing-day afterthought.
